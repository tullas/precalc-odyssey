Polar coordinates: distance and direction

When “how far?” and “which way?” are more natural than “how many blocks east and north.”

Two ways to name a point

Cartesian coordinates (x, y) measure offset along fixed horizontal and vertical axes—great for city grids, spreadsheets, and most algebra graphs. Polar coordinates (r, θ) measure:

  • r — distance from a chosen center (the origin)
  • θ — direction from a fixed reference ray (usually the positive x-axis), often counterclockwise

Neither system is “more true.” Each matches different situations. If you stand at a lighthouse and report a ship, you naturally say range and bearing—polar language—not “847 meters east and 312 meters north” as the first sentence.

Everyday and practical uses

  • Radar and air traffic style reports — distance from the antenna plus angle around the horizon.
  • Game joysticks and analog sticks — how far you push (intensity) and in which direction (angle).
  • Speakers and stage lights — aim by angle; power or brightness often tied to distance from the source.
  • Sprinklers and rotating cameras — fixed or varying reach while sweeping an angle.
  • Circular motion — a point on a Ferris wheel has nearly constant r and changing θ.
  • Graphics and robotics — turning toward a target is often an angle problem; driving forward is an r problem.

When a problem is organized around a center, polar coordinates usually reduce clutter.

Bridge to the map: x and y

To plot on ordinary axes:

x = r · cos θ     y = r · sin θ

In words: cosine picks the east–west share of the distance; sine picks the north–south share. Going the other way, distance is the Pythagorean length:

r = √(x² + y²)

(with θ chosen to match the correct quadrant).

Polar point explorer

Interactive Sliders for r and θ

Try it: Increase r to move outward. Change θ to spin. Watch Cartesian x and y update—those are the conversion formulas in action.

x = r cos θ = 2.60
y = r sin θ = 1.50

Special case: constant r

If every point shares the same r, you are describing a circle centered at the origin. The polar equation r = 6 means “everything six units from the center”—the track a robot follows if it keeps fixed distance from a beacon while angle changes.

Trap: For first intuition, treat r ≥ 0 as distance. Some textbooks allow negative r (meaning “go the opposite direction”); learn the positive-r picture solidly first.
