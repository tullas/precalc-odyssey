Conic sections — a deep guide

One family of curves from geometry, algebra, and the physical world: parabola, circle, ellipse, hyperbola.

1. Where the name comes from

Take a double cone (two ice-cream cones tip-to-tip). Cut it with a flat plane. The edge of the cut is a conic section:

Circle Plane perpendicular to the axis
Ellipse Plane tilted, cuts one nappe completely
Parabola Plane parallel to a side generator
Hyperbola Plane steep enough to cut both nappes

In algebra class the same shapes appear as equations of degree 2 in x and y. Geometry and algebra are two languages for one family.

2. One definition that unifies them: focus and directrix

A powerful definition (Apollonius / modern textbooks):

A conic is the set of points P such that the distance from P to a fixed point (the focus) is a constant multiple of the distance from P to a fixed line (the directrix).

That constant multiple is the eccentricity e:

e = (distance to focus) / (distance to directrix)

Eccentricity eCurveShape intuition
e = 0CircleFocus at center; no useful directrix in the same way
0 < e < 1EllipseClosed oval; “mildly off-center”
e = 1ParabolaOpens forever; balanced focus vs directrix
e > 1HyperbolaTwo branches; focus dominates

So eccentricity is not decoration—it is the knob that turns one definition into four familiar shapes.

3. Parabola — the “equal distance” curve

For a parabola, e = 1: every point is equally far from the focus and the directrix. Standard vertical form centered at the origin:

x² = 4py    or    y = (1/(4p)) x²

Here p is the distance from vertex to focus (and from vertex to directrix). The vertex is the turning point; the axis of symmetry runs through focus and vertex.

Why engineers care about parabolas

  • Satellite dishes and radio telescopes — Parallel incoming rays reflect to the focus. Put a receiver there and you collect weak signals.
  • Flashlights and headlights — Reverse the idea: a bulb at the focus sends out roughly parallel rays.
  • Solar cookers — Same focusing principle for sunlight.
  • Projectile paths (idealized) — Under constant gravity and no air resistance, the path of a thrown ball is a parabola.
  • Some arches and cables — Approximations appear in architecture and suspension structures.

The reflection property is the “use case” most people can picture: energy in, concentrated at one point—or light at one point, sent out as a beam.

4. Ellipse — the closed oval

An ellipse has two foci. A useful equivalent definition: the sum of distances from any point on the ellipse to the two foci is constant (equal to the major-axis length 2a).

Standard form, center at the origin, major axis horizontal:

x²/a² + y²/b² = 1    with   a > b > 0

  • a = semi-major axis (half the long diameter)
  • b = semi-minor axis (half the short diameter)
  • c = √(a² − b²) = distance from center to each focus
  • eccentricity e = c/a (between 0 and 1)

When a = b, c = 0 and e = 0: the ellipse becomes a circle.

Where ellipses appear

  • Planetary orbits — Kepler’s first law: planets orbit in ellipses with the sun at one focus (not the center).
  • Whispering galleries — Sound from one focus reflects to the other focus along many paths of equal total length.
  • Medical lithotripsy — Some devices place the patient and energy source at the two foci of an ellipsoid so shock waves concentrate at the kidney stone.
  • Tilted circles in drawings — A circle viewed at an angle looks elliptical; artists and CAD use that constantly.

5. Circle — the special ellipse

x² + y² = r²

Every point is at fixed distance r from the center. In the eccentricity table, e = 0. Circles are the default for wheels, gears, compass constructions, and “constant distance from a beacon” in polar form (r = constant).

6. Hyperbola — two branches

Eccentricity e > 1. Standard horizontal form:

x²/a² − y²/b² = 1

There are two disconnected branches. Asymptotes y = ±(b/a)x guide the long-run slope. A difference-of-distances definition (difference to two foci is constant) mirrors the ellipse’s sum definition.

Where hyperbolas appear

  • Navigation (historical LORAN-style ideas) — Differences in signal time from two stations place you on a hyperbola.
  • Cooling towers — Hyperboloid shapes (3D relatives) for structural and airflow reasons.
  • Inverse relationships in models — Some xy = constant graphs are rectangular hyperbolas rotated 45°.
  • Gravitational assist / some orbital paths — Unbound paths under inverse-square forces can be hyperbolic.

7. Recognizing equations (quick classification)

For a general second-degree equation

Ax² + Bxy + Cy² + Dx + Ey + F = 0

the discriminant B² − 4AC roughly classifies the type (when the graph is non-degenerate):

  • B² − 4AC < 0 → ellipse (or circle, or empty/point)
  • B² − 4AC = 0 → parabola
  • B² − 4AC > 0 → hyperbola

Completing the square (and rotating if B ≠ 0) turns the equation into a standard form you can graph from a, b, and orientation.

8. How the four curves relate in one sentence

They are all “degree-2 curves” born from the same cone and the same focus–directrix idea; eccentricity decides whether the path closes (ellipse/circle), balances into an open bowl (parabola), or splits into two runaway branches (hyperbola).

Study habit: For each new problem, ask: (1) Which type? (2) Where is the center/vertex? (3) What are a, b, or p? (4) Does a real application (focus, orbit, reflection) match this shape?

Trap: “Oval-looking” is not enough—ellipses have a precise sum-of-distances property. Sketching without checking a, b, and orientation is the main source of exam errors.
